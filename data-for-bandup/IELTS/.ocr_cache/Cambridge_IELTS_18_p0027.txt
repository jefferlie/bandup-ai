E The field is called space traffic management, because it’s similar to managing traffic
on the roads or in the air. Think about a busy day at an airport, says Moriba Jah,
an astrodynamicist at the University of Texas at Austin: planes line up in the sky,

landing and taking off close to one another in a carefully choreographed routine.
Air-traffic controllers know the location of the planes down to one metre in accuracy.
The same can’t be said for space debris. Not all objects in orbit are known, and
even those included in databases are not tracked consistently.

F  Anadditional problem is that there is no authoritative catalogue that accurately
lists the orbits of all known space debris. Jah illustrates this with a web-based
database that he has developed. It draws on several sources, such as catalogues
maintained by the US and Russian governments, to visualise where objects are in
space. When he types in an identifier for a particular space object, the database
draws a purple line to designate its orbit. Only this doesn’t quite work for a number
of objects, such as a Russian rocket body designated in the database as object
number 32280. When Jah enters that number, the database draws two purple lines:
the US and Russian sources contain two completely different orbits for the same
object. Jah says that it is almost impossible to tell which is correct, unless a third
source of information made it possible to cross-correlate.
